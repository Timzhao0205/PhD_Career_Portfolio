## Thesis

The opportunity is a replaceable, shelf-format protection and sequencing unit for an 800-VDC AI rack or its power sidecar. The unit combines a solid-state or hybrid breaker, managed precharge and inrush control, insulation monitoring, hot-swap sequencing, fault and arc discrimination, and useful telemetry behind one interface. The thesis is not that circuit breakers are new. It is that an 800-VDC rack exposes several tightly coupled failure-control functions that become dangerous when separately specified, and that a merchant module could let rack and power-shelf integrators qualify the combined behavior once rather than rediscover it for each platform.

The demand signal is unusually concrete. The Open Compute Project's Mount Diablo work, reported as backed by Google, Meta, and Microsoft, separates compute from a power sidecar and points from 400 VDC toward 800 and 1,500 VDC as rack power rises from roughly 120 kW toward as much as 1 MW. That architecture makes fault isolation, serviceability, controlled connection, and module replacement system-level jobs rather than optional components [L02-044]. In China, an April 2026 800-V power forum co-chaired by Baidu Intelligent Cloud and ByteDance said that 800-V protection logic differs materially from the older 240-V approach and identified unresolved fault-output, grounding, arcing, insulation-monitoring, connector, component, and certification questions [P3R2-C-01-S03]. These sources establish a painful job on both routes.

They do **not** establish that buyers will purchase the proposed all-in-one module from a startup. That missing merchant boundary is the decisive uncertainty. OCP can standardize a fuse-based architecture; a platform owner can allocate protection among its own shelves; or a large power vendor can bundle the functions into a qualified system. The opportunity survives only if at least one class of integrator wants a replaceable, cross-vendor protection layer and will preserve that interface through the 2029 design-in cycle.

## Exact buyers, job, and demand mechanism

The first US buyers are not data-center owners buying a generic breaker from a catalog. They are rack and power-sidecar integrators responsible for an 800-V bus, followed by hyperscaler engineering organizations that approve a reference design. Their exact job is to connect and disconnect a high-energy DC branch without uncontrolled inrush; detect insulation or ground faults; distinguish destructive arcs and short circuits from legitimate accelerator-load transients; interrupt quickly enough to keep one fault from collapsing adjacent loads; sequence blind-mate replacement safely; and expose event records for root-cause analysis. OCP's architecture work is buyer-led evidence that the interface is being defined now [L02-044]. Wolfspeed's 2026 management report of sequential AI-data-center revenue growth and work with ecosystem partners on the 400-to-800-V transition is an upstream confirmation that component qualification activity is live, although it is not a purchase commitment for this unit [L02-047]. GE Vernova's reported surge in data-center electrification orders similarly confirms a large power-infrastructure spending cycle, not the startup's product-level revenue [L08-033].

The China route has a different buyer chain. Likely first counterparties are domestic power-system OEMs, rack integrators, and ODMs serving large compute operators. Baidu and ByteDance's role in the 2026 forum makes the protection problem buyer-adjacent, while the participation of power and rack vendors shows that the issue crosses the supply chain [P3R2-C-01-S03]. Historical deployment evidence matters: Delta, Alibaba, and Zhongheng Electric jointly launched a 240-VDC architecture and deployed it in Alibaba facilities, demonstrating that Chinese operators already co-develop nontraditional DC power systems with suppliers [L02-048]. The later SuperX/Zhongheng launch of two 800-VDC power solutions shows that the domestic market has progressed from discussion to competing system architectures [P3R2-B-22-S01]. That strengthens category demand while weakening any claim of an empty competitive field.

Policy is supporting context rather than a substitute for demand. China's 2024 green-data-center plan set strict efficiency expectations for new large facilities, creating pressure to remove avoidable conversion losses [P3R2-B-01-S01]. The 2026 national AI-energy action plan provides a continuing official framework for compute and energy infrastructure [L14-036], and the 15th Five-Year Plan calls for core-technology breakthroughs through 2030 [P3R2-B-06-S04]. None of those documents orders an 800-V breaker. They explain why operators are investing, whereas the forum and vendor deployments identify the specific job.

## Product boundary and wedge

The v1 should be narrower than a full power shelf and broader than a discrete breaker. Its controlled boundary is one protected DC branch, nominally 800 V and 250–500 A, with five functions:

1. a solid-state or hybrid interruption path and energy-absorption network;
2. managed precharge and discharge so bus capacitance does not create destructive inrush;
3. insulation and ground-fault monitoring coordinated with trip logic;
4. hot-swap state control, interlocks, and connector sequencing; and
5. local waveform capture, event classification, health telemetry, and a deterministic host interface.

The startup should not initially build the upstream rectifier, transformer, rack busbar, point-of-load conversion, cooling system, or campus supervisory controller. It should not claim that one enclosure protects an entire 1-MW rack. A 250–500-A branch at 800 V carries 200–400 kW before derating, so the largest racks would use several protected branches or a higher-current later product. The narrow branch boundary makes a founder-scale brassboard possible and gives an integrator a unit it can test independently.

The integration wedge is non-cosmetic only if coordination measurably reduces failure risk or qualification effort. Precharge current, insulation state, connector position, load permissives, and breaker state must be part of one state machine. Fault classifiers must use the same current and voltage history that drives interruption. Telemetry should capture pre-fault and trip waveforms in a form an operator can compare across a fleet. The defensible asset would be a library of real transient and fault signatures plus verified control policies—not ownership of SiC or GaN physics.

## Physics and build path

The base physics is credible. Peer-reviewed work has demonstrated SiC-based low-voltage DC solid-state breakers with the necessary drive, detection, snubber, and absorption functions in a tested 400-V prototype [L08-018]. Later work describes adaptive current limiting and millisecond-level self-healing for DC distribution, explicitly including data-center cascading-failure protection as an application [L08-017]. Research on hybrid DC breakers shows how topology choices trade conduction loss, commutation stress, and the number of interruption elements [L08-001]. Work on fault-current limiting shows that a breaker cannot be sized in isolation from the electrical and thermal stress imposed during interruption [L08-003]. Protection research using time-frequency features illustrates a path for separating internal DC faults from external disturbances [L08-004]. These studies clear the science gate; most are not demonstrations of the exact 800-V rack product.

The high-power converter context is also established. A peer-reviewed 15-kW, 500-kHz DC/DC stage was developed as a module for a 225-kW medium-voltage-to-400-VDC system, illustrating the packaging, isolation, and modular-conversion environment around a DC rack bus [L02-001]. GaN reliability literature documents trapping-induced increases in on-resistance, a reminder that fast devices must be qualified across temperature, voltage stress, and switching history rather than selected from headline efficiency alone [L02-013]. Innoscience says its own GaN portfolio spans the 800-to-1-V conversion chain for NVIDIA's ecosystem [P3R2-C-01-S01], and independent Chinese reporting places the company in that 800-V supply ecosystem [L02-050]. Those sources make a domestic-device China bill of materials plausible, but they do not validate a high-current breaker design.

A sensible build sequence is therefore staged. First, use a low-energy hardware-in-the-loop and switched-capacitor emulator to develop state logic, sensors, waveform capture, and nuisance-trip discrimination. Second, build a 400-V limited-energy pole to measure commutation, overvoltage, thermal behavior, and failure containment. Third, move to an 800-V/250-A brassboard in a suitable high-power laboratory with remote operation, blast containment, an external emergency disconnect, and independent safety review. Fourth, run endurance and abnormal-state campaigns: repeated precharge, loss of auxiliary power, welded bypass contact, sensor drift, insulation degradation, connector bounce, load-step transients, and hot-swap sequences. Full interruption at target available fault current and thousands of mechanical service cycles cannot be honestly validated on a $48,000 budget.

## Incumbents and the strongest bear case

The bear case is product-boundary absorption, not impossible physics. Large power vendors can combine conversion, switchgear, protection, firmware, certification, and field service. Platform owners can specify where fuses or breakers sit and remove the need for an independent merchant shelf. Domestic Chinese vendors are already launching 800-V systems [P3R2-B-22-S01]. Innoscience can enable locally sourced power stages [P3R2-C-01-S01]. The TMTPost architecture review describes competing 800-V conversion approaches and emphasizes semiconductor voltage, resonant conversion, and isolation challenges, showing that the supply chain is attacking the full stack rather than waiting for one protection startup [L02-054].

The proposed company does not possess a credible 10x edge merely because its switch clears in under 100 microseconds. Solid-state competitors are also fast, and topology research is widely available [L08-017; L08-018]. A fuse may remain cheaper, simpler, and acceptable where service replacement is infrequent. The 10x case must instead be tested at the system outcome level: compared with the buyer's baseline, does the integrated unit reduce fault-clearing time by at least an order of magnitude **without** increasing nuisance trips; cut recovery or root-cause time by at least 10x through event capture; or reduce protection-integration and qualification effort by at least 10x across repeated rack designs? If none of those outcomes is demonstrated, “integration” is convenience, not a durable wedge.

The most damaging scenario is straightforward. By 2028, OCP and major platform specifications converge on protection allocation; incumbent shelf makers certify compliant offerings; integrators treat protection firmware as proprietary; and a 2030 startup can sell only an interchangeable breaker element into a price-driven market. The score of 59.6 and marginal G4/G7 verdicts correctly retain this risk.

## Decisive experiment: merchant-module boundary falsification

The first experiment is commercial and architectural because building power hardware before proving the socket would answer the wrong question. Budget: **$48,000 over four months**. No signed access, paid evaluation, or purchase order is assumed.

The work package is: $10,000 for specification mapping; $6,000 for a fault-mode, effects, and safety case; $8,000 for a proposed interface-control document; $8,000 for structured integrator workshops; $8,000 for China counterparty screening and counsel; $4,000 for evaluation contracting; and $4,000 for independent review. Before outreach, freeze the proposed module boundary, required signals, mechanical envelope assumptions, evidence forms, and 2029 design-in horizon. Approach two US OCP-aligned power integrators and one screened China ODM or power OEM. Verbal enthusiasm earns no credit.

**Pass:** two integrators provide written requirement redlines, and at least one counterparty signs a paid evaluation that assigns breaker, precharge, insulation monitoring, and sequencing to a replaceable third-party module through 2029. The agreement may be contingent on later technical milestones; it must define an interface and evaluation deliverables.

**Kill:** all counterparties allocate the functions to fuses, internal shelves, or platform-owned specifications; nobody will redline the interface; or no counterparty will pay for evaluation of a replaceable module. A pass does not prove the breaker works. It only justifies the next roughly $350,000 technical brassboard. A kill should stop the company thesis even if the founder can build excellent hardware.

## Bottom-up commercial arithmetic

There is no validated per-unit price in the evidence pack, so the arithmetic must remain an explicit scenario. At 800 V, a 250-A branch is 200 kW and a 500-A branch is 400 kW. Allowing for redundancy, derating, and architecture, assume **one to four modules per rack/sidecar pair**. Assume an early qualified-module average selling price of **$12,000–$25,000**; this is a planning input, not sourced market data.

A 1,000-rack program would then require 1,000–4,000 units and represent $12 million–$100 million of module revenue over its buildout. A smaller 100-rack qualification deployment would represent 100–400 units and $1.2 million–$10 million. At an illustrative 45% gross margin, the latter contributes only $0.54 million–$4.5 million before certification, applications engineering, warranty reserves, and field support. The enormous range is intentional: branch count, redundancy, and the decision to centralize protection dominate more than campus headlines.

The evidence justifies using a large underlying spend pool—OCP describes power sidecars for rapidly rising rack power [L02-044], and major suppliers report strong AI and data-center power activity [L02-047; L08-033]. It does not justify an assumed share. Commercial diligence must obtain three numbers from actual redlines: protected branches per rack, incumbent bill-of-material cost, and acceptable premium for resettable protection and diagnostics. If the acceptable ASP is below the cost of qualified power semiconductors, containment, sensors, and service, the revenue scenario collapses.

## Separate US and China routes

**US route.** From 2026 through 2027, participate in the OCP power workstream, publish topology and transient benchmarks that do not disclose core control IP, and use the $48,000 experiment to secure a written interface and paid evaluation. In 2027–28, build the limited-energy pole and 800-V brassboard with a US integrator's waveform envelope. In 2028–29, pursue pre-compliance, reliability evidence, and one design-in. The US offer should emphasize an embeddable module, documented functional-safety behavior, event telemetry, and rapid applications support. Do not start with a full shelf competing head-on with incumbent power-system suppliers.

**China route.** Use a separately governed Chinese entity or tightly scoped licensee, a domestic device bill of materials, local manufacturing and safety engineering, and an OEM/ODM design-in. The 2026 forum identifies unresolved domestic standards and component gaps [P3R2-C-01-S03], while existing deployments prove that operators work through domestic power partners [L02-048; P3R2-B-22-S01]. The lack of a settled 800-V certification standard is not automatic whitespace: it may delay qualification or let large incumbents shape the standard. China should therefore begin with a paid standards/interface study and laboratory evaluation, not an assumption of cross-border direct sales.

The two routes should share abstract requirements, test methods, and non-sensitive learning where lawful, but use separate controlled designs and device sourcing. China's official 2023 regime requires licensing for covered gallium- and germanium-related exports and names GaN-relevant materials; it is a licensing constraint, not an outright prohibition [P3R2-C-01-S02]. That makes a domestic China supply chain and careful technology-control plan base-case operating costs. The venture must not promise identical hardware across the two markets.

## Safety, compliance, and reliability

An 800-VDC bus is lethal and can sustain arcs that do not benefit from AC current zero-crossings. Safety engineering begins before topology optimization. Required controls include galvanic and mechanical isolation; creepage and clearance analysis; touch-safe terminals; controlled discharge; redundant state sensing; fail-safe auxiliary-power behavior; fault-energy containment; safe service interlocks; and external emergency isolation during development. Test plans must distinguish component short, open, degraded switching, sensor disagreement, insulation fault, connector fault, and controller failure.

Compliance work should map the applicable power-electronic converter, switchgear, rack-system, electromagnetic-compatibility, insulation-coordination, and functional-safety requirements with a qualified laboratory. “Pre-certified” must never mean that a component-level report automatically certifies the rack. China's stated absence of a dedicated 800-V safety framework in 2026 increases the need for early authority and laboratory engagement [P3R2-C-01-S03]. Reliability proof must include semiconductor degradation, thermal cycling, repeated interruption, hot-swap endurance, nuisance-trip rate, telemetry integrity, and safe recovery after firmware faults. GaN trapping evidence reinforces the need to test dynamic on-resistance and aging rather than extrapolate from new-device curves [L02-013].

## Milestones and kill dates

**2026–2027:** freeze the merchant boundary; complete the $48,000 falsification; join the relevant US workstream; engage the China standards and OEM ecosystem separately; publish a comparative protection benchmark; and build a low-energy state-machine demonstrator. Kill if no paid interface evaluation emerges.

**2027–2028:** complete 400-V and then 800-V staged hardware; demonstrate controlled precharge, safe discharge, deterministic sequencing, contained interruption, and discrimination on counterparty-supplied load envelopes. Kill if the device cannot clear within the agreed protection envelope without unacceptable overvoltage, thermal stress, or nuisance trips.

**2028–2029:** run endurance and pre-compliance campaigns; obtain a written design-in path with at least one US integrator and one independent China route if both remain viable. Lock separate bills of materials and governance. Kill the 2030 launch if the functions have been absorbed into platform specifications or if certification cost and time exceed the company's financing plan.

**2030 launch:** sell an evaluation-qualified branch module and engineering kit, not an unqualified universal rack breaker. A realistic v1 company may require **$8 million–$20 million** once certification, tooling, inventory, applications engineering, and warranty support are included; that is materially beyond the founder's prototype budget and should remain an explicit financing gate.

**2030–2034:** expand current ratings and telemetry only after field data. The durable path is to become the verified protection interface across successive rack generations. The kill date is 2034: if repeat designs, field reliability, and a cross-vendor interface have not appeared by then, the business is an applications-heavy custom integrator rather than a scalable product company.

## Evidence durability and refresh plan

Durable evidence includes the physics of DC interruption, fault-current stress, fault classification, power-device reliability, and modular DC conversion [L08-001; L08-003; L08-004; L08-017; L08-018; L02-001; L02-013]. China's licensing regime and official planning horizon are also durable constraints, though implementation details can change [P3R2-C-01-S02; P3R2-B-06-S04].

Refresh-sensitive evidence includes OCP voltage and interface revisions, named ecosystem partners, rack-power forecasts, supplier revenue momentum, 800-V product launches, Chinese standards gaps, device availability, pricing, and every competitor claim [L02-044; L02-047; L08-033; L02-054; L02-048; L02-050; P3R2-C-01-S03; P3R2-B-22-S01; P3R2-C-01-S01]. Recheck these in 2027 and again before any 2029 incorporation decision. The thesis fails if specifications eliminate the module boundary even while the overall 800-V market grows.

## Verdict

**Conditional pursue / HOLD, consistent with the frozen selection.** The idea deserves a top-10 deep dive because US and China evidence independently confirms a real 800-V protection, grounding, fault, and serviceability problem; the physics is buildable; and the work matches founder-scale circuits, controls, embedded systems, and data acquisition. It does not deserve a higher-confidence verdict because the exact merchant socket, 10x buyer outcome, qualification burden, and 2030 competitive window remain unproven.

Spend the first $48,000 to falsify the product boundary. Proceed to the high-energy brassboard only after a written, paid evaluation preserves a replaceable module through 2029. If integrators instead standardize fuses, internal shelves, or platform-owned protection, kill promptly. The attractive market can be real while this startup is still the wrong company.
