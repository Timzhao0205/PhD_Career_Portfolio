## Decision snapshot

**Frozen selection status:** rank 9 of 24; selected for a deep dive. **Frozen score:** 54.6, high confidence. **Frozen gates:** G1 pass; G2 pass; G3 pass; G4 pass_marginal; G5 pass; G6 pass; G7 pass. This deep dive does not rescore or overwrite those decisions. It explains why the idea remains attractive after red-team pressure and what must be true before capital is committed.

The thesis is not “invent electronics that can briefly switch at 300°C.” Research has already shown much hotter SiC operation, while recent SOI work gives circuit-design criteria at 300°C [L15-009; L15-004]. The venture thesis is narrower and harder: create a reproducible, qualified acquisition front end—ADC, multiplexer, reference, drivers, passives, interconnect, and package—that holds calibrated performance for months at 300°C and can be designed into tools by more than one US customer. The commercial product should begin as a qualified module and paid engineering service on an accessible process. Custom merchant silicon is a later step, earned only after a foundry-continuity agreement and two customer evaluation programs.

The opportunity exists because the temperature demanded by superhot-resource programs is moving beyond catalog electronics just as legacy high-temperature supply has weakened. ARPA-E’s $30 million SUPERHOT program targets reservoirs above 375°C and 22 MPa and frames 10–20 GW of accessible baseload potential [L15-029; P3R2-A-14-S02]. In parallel, CISSOID says its 175–225°C line entered last-time-buy after termination of the underlying foundry process, with the ordering window closed and only inventory being managed [P3R2-A-14-S04]. That juxtaposition is compelling, but it is not a purchase order for this product. The case survives only if engineering demand can be converted into paid evaluation and if the startup can avoid repeating CISSOID’s process-dependency failure.

## Buyer, painful job, and evidence of demand

The initial buyer is a US downhole-tool or geothermal instrumentation organization, not a geothermal power-plant owner buying bare chips. Likely technical sponsors sit in drilling, logging, completions, condition monitoring, or advanced-tool engineering at an oilfield-service company; program-backed tool developers are a second route. Their job is to collect trustworthy pressure, vibration, temperature, position, or weak-signal measurements while the local electronics face temperatures beyond ordinary silicon ratings. Today they must combine limited-temperature electronics with thermal isolation, flasks, duty cycling, remote placement, or custom in-house designs. Each workaround consumes tool volume, limits operating time, complicates calibration, or postpones measurement until a cooler part of the operation.

Demand evidence is strong at the host-system level. Baker Hughes and XGS announced collaboration on a 150 MW New Mexico geothermal project with an exploration and engineering phase and a 2030 full-operation target [P3R2-A-14-S01]. Baker Hughes separately reports substantial company-wide R&D and New Energy orders, including equipment for five organic-Rankine-cycle plants totaling 300 MW and subsurface drilling and production work [L15-040]. SLB and Ormat announced an enhanced-geothermal pilot at an existing site intended to de-risk later commercial deployment [L15-041], with independent reporting describing a pilot-to-scale pathway and named customer categories [P3R2-A-14-S06]. DOE’s FORGE awards identify real performers working on high-temperature isolation tools, stimulation, monitoring, proppants, and packers [L15-027; L15-028]. These records show funded wells and tool-development activity, not a generic market forecast.

Government technical demand is even more explicit about temperature. DARPA’s THERMAL solicitation specifies mixed-signal SiC/GaN building blocks up to 800°C, including a Phase II ADC target above 10,000 samples per second, 8-bit resolution, under 1 W, and over 40 dB SNR [L15-026; P3R2-A-14-S05]. NASA’s HOTTech program and GEER testing sustain a separate institutional route for electronics designed for long-duration extreme-temperature operation [L15-030]. The technical need is therefore credible and multi-institutional.

The evidence gap is product-specific commitment. None of the geothermal project records specifies a merchant 300°C acquisition module, pays this proposed company, or establishes a design-in. No signed access, letter of intent, or purchase order is assumed. This distinction matters because tool majors may satisfy the same need with thermal management, fiber sensing, or internal electronics. The immediate commercial milestone is consequently not “the sector is growing”; it is two independent, conditional paid evaluation or NRE agreements tied to an interface, a test fixture, and acceptance metrics. At least one should be with a tool OEM rather than a research program.

## Product boundary: what the company builds

The minimum product is an eight-channel, 300°C-class sensor-acquisition module. It includes a high-temperature analog front end, input multiplexing, a stable reference, conversion, clocking or timing support, local health telemetry, high-temperature passives, interconnect, die attach, ceramic packaging, and documented calibration compensation across temperature. It exposes a defined electrical and mechanical interface so a customer can evaluate it without adopting a new full logging architecture.

The boundary deliberately excludes a complete measurement-while-drilling tool, pressure housing, turbine controller, sensor portfolio, communications stack, and surface analytics system. It also excludes an immediate broad catalog of high-temperature ICs. Building the full tool would collide with vertically integrated service companies and bury the differentiator under mechanics, telemetry, and field service. Selling only an unpackaged die would leave the hardest reliability problem with the customer. The module-plus-NRE wedge keeps ownership of the high-temperature signal chain and package while allowing a customer to retain its tool IP.

The first release can partition functions. The hot-zone front end handles sensing, multiplexing, reference, and conversion; computation that cannot yet survive at 300°C can remain behind thermal isolation or farther up the tool. This reduces the circuit set that needs extreme-temperature qualification. A chipset becomes rational only when recurring volume, stable models, and cross-customer interfaces justify it. The evidence from high-temperature SOI design indicates that 300°C operation requires attention to leakage and reduced current density, not merely nominal room-temperature functionality [L15-004]. The company is therefore selling a qualified operating envelope and reliability data package as much as circuitry.

## Physics, build path, and packaging bottleneck

The underlying semiconductor physics is supportive. SiC offers wide-bandgap, high-temperature potential far beyond conventional silicon, although commercial devices and complete circuits remain constrained by gate drive, sensing, model matching, and packaging [L15-001]. NASA Glenn demonstrated prolonged SiC integrated-circuit operation in a roughly 460°C, 9.4 MPa simulated environment [L15-009]. This makes a 300°C target scientifically plausible. It does not make an eight-channel precision acquisition module straightforward: oscillator operation is different from low-drift analog conversion, and a die demonstration is different from a manufacturable assembly.

The build program has four coupled workstreams. First, circuit design must characterize device models, leakage, reference drift, noise, matching, electromigration, and conversion linearity over temperature. Second, known-good die must be screened so package failures are not confused with die defects. Third, the assembly must combine ceramic substrate, metallization, die attach, wire bonds or alternative interconnect, passives, feedthrough interfaces, and sealing. Fourth, firmware or calibration tables must compensate repeatable temperature effects without hiding irreversible degradation.

Packaging is the gating technical asset. Sintered-silver die attach is a credible high-temperature option, but the literature emphasizes mechanical characterization under storage, thermal shock, and cycling rather than a universal drop-in solution [L15-005]. SiC power-cycling research likewise shows that interfaces and package fatigue remain central reliability mechanisms [L15-025]. The venture should therefore maintain failure-analysis capability and a materials/process “traveler” for every coupon and module: paste lot, pressure profile, cure, substrate finish, bond parameters, seal, thermal history, and electrical baselines. The defensible database is the link between process variables and drift/failure, not merely a circuit schematic.

Adjacent evidence should not be overextended. Radiation testing of a commercial robotic manipulator found a component-level failure and illustrates that planned replacement can sometimes beat specialized hardening [L15-002]; that is a useful warning that customers may choose replaceable standard components, not proof of 300°C demand. Chinese petroleum-logging research identifies weak-signal detection, high-temperature tolerance, modeling, and IP constraints [L15-003]; it supports the global engineering problem but does not establish a countable Chinese buyer for this product. These sources belong in the technical context, not in US revenue arithmetic.

## Incumbents, substitutes, and the strongest bear case

Catalog incumbents and substitutes include remaining high-temperature silicon lines, CISSOID inventory, specialist IC design houses, and internal electronics teams at large tool companies. Research programs have also produced substantial 300°C circuit and system work. DARPA performers could leapfrog the proposed operating point, while NASA-derived SiC capabilities may seed specialist suppliers. The frozen G4 verdict is therefore pass_marginal: the whitespace is not “no one can build at 300°C.” It is the absence of an evidently available, multi-customer, qualified acquisition module with a durable process and package.

The strongest bear case is economic and organizational. CISSOID’s exit may show a temporary supply gap, or it may show that niche volume cannot retain a foundry process [P3R2-A-14-S04]. A startup without a named PDK, multi-project-wafer cadence, minimum wafer terms, model ownership, and a continuity plan cannot promise ten-year industrial availability. Even a perfect 1,000-hour soak does not prove wafer yield, lot-to-lot drift, pressure feedthrough reliability, field shock survival, or a customer design-in. Large tool companies can build only the channels they need, purchase NRE from an established semiconductor partner, or continue using insulated electronics. If the accessible annual market is tens rather than hundreds of modules, a broad platform cannot amortize $8–18 million of v1 capital.

The company should respond by staying asset-light initially. It licenses or contracts an existing SiC/SOI flow, buys MPW or small wafer lots, qualifies a package, and charges NRE plus module revenue. It does not finance a proprietary process before customer evidence. A second-source process need not be electrically identical at first, but the architecture, high-temperature models, calibration method, and package should be portable enough that loss of one foundry does not end the company.

## The 10× claim and how to test it

“Works at 300°C” is a capability jump over a 225°C catalog ceiling, but it is not automatically a tenfold buyer benefit. The buyer-valued claim must be one of these: at least ten times longer useful hot-zone measurement duration; at least ten times lower calibration drift over a specified run; elimination of a thermal flask that frees enough tool volume for another measurement; or a step change in data continuity that avoids a repeat tool run. The selected technical target—1,000 continuous hours at 300°C with less than 1% drift—creates a clear qualification datum. It must be compared with the customer’s present architecture under the same sensor input and thermal profile.

The decisive campaign is the frozen 2028 experiment: an eight-channel SiC/SOI front end completes a 1,000-hour powered soak at 300°C with less than 1% drift, independently witnessed, while the package stack survives 500 thermal cycles. The frozen budget is **$850,000**. That is above the portfolio’s preferred experiment band but is realistic for design, fabrication, packaging iterations, environmental equipment, metrology, and independent witnessing. Pass criteria should also include no channel loss, bounded integral and differential nonlinearity, noise and reference stability, hermeticity, and post-test destructive analysis. The module must be tested across a statistically meaningful build rather than one hero unit.

Before that campaign, a **$75,000, 12-week veto stage** should require three things: a written foundry offer covering PDK/model access, MPW or wafer terms, and a credible 2028–2034 continuity path; two independent conditional paid-evaluation or NRE term sheets; and at least 20 package coupons completing the prescribed thermal cycle with no more than 10% failures and prespecified resistance, leakage, hermeticity, and drift limits. Passing this stage does not validate the company and must not be counted as a decisive sub-$100,000 experiment. It only licenses the $850,000 campaign. Lack of foundry terms, fewer than two customer commitments, or coupon failure above threshold is a kill.

Access is a real condition, not an assumed asset. The plan requires contracted wafer access, a laboratory capable of long powered soaks and cycling, independent test witnessing, known-good die, failure analysis, and customer-approved electrical and mechanical interfaces. No existing signed access to any of these is claimed.

## Bottom-up commercial arithmetic

No reliable price exists for a product that is not commercially established, so the model uses explicit scenarios rather than a top-down market report. Assume a module average selling price of $75,000–$200,000, reflecting low volume, qualification data, and packaging; assume paid NRE of $250,000–$1 million for an initial customer integration. These are planning assumptions, not observed prices.

A conservative early portfolio might be three customers, each paying $500,000 of NRE and buying 10 modules per year at $100,000. That yields $1.5 million of one-time NRE plus $3 million annual module revenue. A base case of five customers buying 25 modules annually at $125,000 produces $15.6 million of annual module revenue, plus integration work. An upside case of eight customers buying 50 modules at $150,000 produces $60 million annually. The range is consistent with a specialized industrial supplier, not a mass semiconductor company.

The host-project evidence places a ceiling on enthusiasm rather than validating unit counts. SUPERHOT’s 10–20 GW objective demonstrates strategic scale [P3R2-A-14-S02], and FORGE awards demonstrate repeated tool-development work [L15-027; L15-028], but neither specifies modules per well. The earlier 100–300-well extrapolation is too uncertain to underwrite a fab-like cost structure. The venture is financeable only if NRE and module gross margin carry the early organization and if the same qualified core can serve multiple instruments without extensive one-off redesign.

## Geography and route to market

The United States is the only countable beachhead. The route begins with government-backed test infrastructure and tool developers, then moves into a paid evaluation with a service-company engineering group. FORGE and SUPERHOT provide communities and representative conditions; the Baker Hughes/XGS and SLB/Ormat programs supply dated commercial context [L15-029; P3R2-A-14-S01; L15-041]. A good 2029 outcome is not broad procurement. It is one witnessed field-representative test, two paid evaluations, an agreed qualification matrix, and one repeat module order.

China remains **non-countable**. A peer-reviewed logging source documents relevant technical pain [L15-003], but the evidence pack contains no named Chinese procurement for a 300°C merchant acquisition module and no qualifying buyer commitment. China must not be added to revenue, customer, market-count, or timing claims without new country-specific primary evidence. The company may monitor technical literature, but the frozen selection’s China beachhead remains false.

## Safety, compliance, and qualification

This is not primarily a regulatory-approval company, but safety and industrial qualification cannot be postponed. High-temperature test rigs require guarded hot zones, over-temperature shutdown, controlled pressure vessels where used, inerting or ventilation for materials outgassing, traceable calibration, electrical isolation, and documented handling of failed packages. A field-representative assembly needs pressure-boundary separation so electronics failure cannot compromise well control. Failure-mode-and-effects analysis should cover short circuits, sensor saturation, reference drift, intermittent interconnects, seal loss, and misleading but plausible data.

Compliance work should include customer-specific qualification, quality-system controls, lot traceability, materials declarations, and an export-classification review before any cross-border shipment. The frozen G6 pass is preserved: no special export regime is assumed to block the US beachhead. That verdict does not waive review if defense-funded know-how, controlled technical data, or a future application changes the classification. NASA HOTTech and DARPA THERMAL evidence should be used as technical benchmarks, not as permission to transfer program-restricted IP [L15-030; L15-026].

## Roadmap and kill logic

**2026–2027:** Choose one accessible SiC or SOI process; secure written PDK, MPW/wafer, modeling, and continuity terms. Freeze the module interface with two prospective evaluators. Fabricate simple references, multiplexers, amplifiers, oscillators, and package coupons. Complete the $75,000 veto stage. Kill if foundry continuity is purely verbal, if no two conditional paid evaluations emerge, or if package coupon yield misses threshold.

**2028–2029:** Execute the frozen $850,000 qualification campaign: 1,000 powered hours at 300°C, less than 1% drift, and 500 package thermal cycles, with independent witnessing and a multi-unit sample. Follow with a heated autoclave or test-well demonstration using a customer-relevant sensor and interface. Obtain paid NRE, not an unsupported expression of interest. Kill if results depend on cherry-picked units, if lot-to-lot behavior is unstable, or if a customer can match performance using a simpler thermal workaround.

**2030–2034:** Launch a qualified module and engineering-data package into the US beachhead. Expand channel count or sensor interfaces only when repeat customer demand supports them. Consider custom silicon after at least two customers reorder and process continuity is contractually credible. A 400°C tier is a roadmap option, not part of v1. Kill or convert to a small engineering-services business if superhot programs slip, annual demand remains below roughly 30 modules, the platform requires customer-specific redesign every time, or an incumbent offers equivalent qualified modules with a more durable supply chain.

## Durable facts versus refresh-sensitive claims

Durable facts include the wide-bandgap temperature advantage, demonstrated SiC operation well above 300°C, leakage and electromigration challenges in SOI at 300°C, and package-interface fatigue as a central reliability problem [L15-001; L15-009; L15-004; L15-005; L15-025]. The need for traceable qualification and the logic of partitioning a hot front end from cooler computation are also durable.

Refresh-sensitive claims include the status and awardees of SUPERHOT, FORGE schedules, the 2030 New Mexico project date, SLB/Ormat pilot progress, DARPA performer status, foundry availability, CISSOID remaining inventory, and every assumed module price or annual unit count [L15-029; L15-027; L15-028; P3R2-A-14-S01; L15-041; P3R2-A-14-S04]. These should be rechecked before financing, customer outreach, and each annual portfolio review. The biggest refresh item is whether any vendor or tool major has introduced a qualified 300°C acquisition module; that could either validate purchasing demand or close the whitespace.

## Verdict

**Proceed conditionally as a module/NRE company; do not begin as a broad semiconductor platform.** The physics is real, the US host-system demand and 2028–2034 timing are credible, and legacy supply contraction creates an opening. The venture’s edge would be a transferable high-temperature package/process database plus independently witnessed low-drift performance—not the bare fact that a circuit toggles at 300°C. The frozen 54.6 score and pass_marginal competition gate correctly reflect a meaningful but contested niche.

The next capital decision is binary. Advance only after written foundry continuity, two independent conditional paid evaluations, and a passing package-coupon campaign. Then fund the $850,000 1,000-hour/500-cycle experiment. If any of those prerequisites fails by the end of 2028, kill the platform thesis or retain only a bounded engineering-services option. China remains non-countable, and no signed customer access or purchase commitment is assumed.
